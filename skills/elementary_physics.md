# Elementary physics

When asked a basic physics question (forces, motion, energy, simple machines,
light, sound, electricity, magnetism, states of matter):

1. Answer in plain, concrete language -- no formulas or jargon unless asked
   for them. Lead with a relatable everyday example (a ball rolling, a light
   switch, a fridge magnet) before any abstract definition.
2. Ground answers in these core facts rather than guessing -- a small local
   model gets physics half-right with real confidence:
   - Force: a push or pull. Unbalanced forces change an object's speed or
     direction; balanced forces mean no change.
   - Gravity: pulls everything toward Earth's center; heavier and lighter
     objects fall at the same rate once you ignore air resistance.
   - Energy: comes in forms (kinetic = motion, potential = stored, e.g. a
     stretched spring or something held up high) and changes form but isn't
     created or destroyed.
   - Simple machines (lever, pulley, wheel & axle, inclined plane, wedge,
     screw) make a task easier by changing the amount or direction of force
     needed, not by creating extra energy.
   - Light travels in straight lines, reflects off surfaces (mirrors), and
     bends (refracts) through water/lenses; it's how we see.
   - Sound is vibration traveling through a medium (air, water, solid) -- it
     needs a medium, so it can't travel through empty space.
   - Electricity flows in a complete circuit (loop); a break anywhere stops
     it. Static electricity is a buildup of charge; current electricity is
     charge flowing.
   - Magnetism: magnets attract iron/nickel/cobalt and other magnets
     (opposite poles attract, like poles repel); Earth itself acts like a
     giant magnet.
   - States of matter: solid (fixed shape), liquid (fixed volume, takes the
     container's shape), gas (fills its container) -- heating/cooling moves
     matter between states.
3. Correct a common misconception directly if the question implies one (e.g.
   "heavier things fall faster" -- no, air resistance is the usual reason
   they seem to).
4. For an exact number (e.g. the speed of light), give the standard value
   plainly rather than approximating from memory of a similar-sounding
   number.
5. If genuinely unsure of a fact, say so instead of guessing -- same rule as
   everywhere else here.
